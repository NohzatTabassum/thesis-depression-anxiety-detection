Thesis title: AI-Assisted Early Risk Detection of Depression and Anxiety in University Students During Job Search Using Social, Behavioral, and Self-Report Signals

1. Introduction
1.1 Background and Context
The transition from academic environments to professional employment constitutes a period of significant psychological vulnerability for university students [REF]. Evidence indicates that university students in the school-to-work transition encounter distinct challenges compared to mature workers, particularly regarding the development of an occupational identity [REF]. This phase frequently generates elevated levels of psychological strain due to the inherent uncertainty of labor market entry [REF]. The anticipation of potential failure acts as a primary stressor, often producing distress levels comparable to the actual experience of joblessness [REF]. Such strain derives from the deprivation of specific benefits, including financial rewards and intangible factors like daily routines or social interaction networks [REF].
Standard screening methods typically rely on isolated psychometric instruments to evaluate symptom severity [REF]. However, comprehensive risk identification requires the integration of multiple data streams to capture the complexity of a student's current risk state [REF]. Behavioral indicators, such as the intensity of job-seeking activities, associate with fluctuations in psychological well-being [REF]. High search effort often correlates with decreased mental health during the unemployment period because the process involves frequent rejection [REF]. External resources, specifically perceived social support from family and friends, function as essential buffers against the onset of clinical anxiety and depression [REF]. Combining these diverse signals into a unified computational framework enables the identification of patterns that often elude human observation [REF].
Within a software engineering context, this research focuses on the design of a reproducible and modular prediction pipeline [REF]. The proposed system utilizes supervised machine learning baselines to process integrated feature sets encompassing social, behavioral, and psychometric signals [REF]. This computational approach functions strictly as an early indication system rather than a clinical diagnostic tool [REF]. By providing interpretable risk indications through explainability mechanisms, the framework serves as a decision-support signal for non-clinical interventions [REF]. This modular architecture ensures the evaluation rigor and deployment feasibility necessary for integration into academic support environments [REF]. Such automated systems offer a cost-effective method to provide personalized support to student populations during high-stress career transitions [REF].

1.2 Problem Statement
The transition from higher education to professional employment exposes students to heightened psychological strain derived from persistent labor market uncertainty [REF]. While standardized screening tools like the GAD-7 quantify symptom severity, these isolated measures fail to capture the behavioral nuances inherent in the job-search process [REF]. Current computational methods exhibit several technical deficiencies, primarily the reliance on single-source data streams that ignore the buffering effects of perceived social support [REF]. Most existing models function as opaque architectures, prioritizing raw classification performance while omitting the explainability necessary for non-clinical intervention [REF]. Additionally, reporting inconsistencies regarding model development and internal validation prevent the cross-cohort reproducibility required for stable deployment [REF]. Failure to integrate current search indicators, such as intensity and strategy, prevents the identification of distress trajectories before symptoms escalate [REF]. This delayed identification of psychological vulnerability often results in exacerbated functional impairment and increased disability days during the school-to-work transition [REF]. Technically, the lack of an integrated pipeline combining psychometric, social, and behavioral signals limits the predictive depth of early indication systems [REF]. Current risk detection methodologies also suffer from significant class imbalances, which systematically distort performance estimates [REF]. Consequently, academic institutions remain unable to provide targeted, data-informed support for students at high risk of anxiety or depression [REF].

2. Literature Review
2.1 Conceptual Foundations: Depression, Anxiety, and Risk Screening
In university settings, depression and anxiety signify multifaceted psychological constructs often operationalized through severity levels rather than formal clinical states [REF]. Supervised learning systems require these definitions to establish ground truth for predictive modeling tasks [REF]. The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) represents a primary instrument for depression screening [REF]. It evaluates nine specific criteria aligned with formal clinical standards to quantify symptom frequency during a 14-day interval [REF]. Scores on the PHQ-9 provide a numeric range from 0 to 27 [REF]. This enables risk categorization into varying severity levels such as mild, moderate, or severe [REF]. Statistical evidence confirms that a threshold of 10 demonstrates 88% sensitivity for identifying major depressive episodes [REF].
Complementing this, the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) functions as a reliable scale for measuring anxiety risk [REF]. This self-report tool identifies probable cases of generalized anxiety through seven items scored on a four-point scale [REF]. Increasing GAD-7 totals associate strongly with functional impairment and elevated healthcare utilization [REF]. Although anxiety and depression symptoms frequently overlap, factor analysis confirms these as distinct clinical dimensions suitable for multi-label classification [REF].
The social environment influences current risk states via specific buffering mechanisms [REF]. Perceived social support, quantified by the MSPSS, accounts for variations in how students internalize stressors during career transitions [REF]. High support levels from family and friends maintain a significant inverse association with distress [REF]. For computational pipelines, these social metrics serve as stable feature sets () that enhance the detection of psychological vulnerability [REF].
Distinguishing automated screening from clinical diagnosis remains mandatory for modular system design [REF]. Screening focuses on estimating the probability that a risk condition is currently present () [REF]. In contrast, clinical diagnosis involves structured interviews by professionals to confirm formal disorders [REF]. By utilizing validated scales to generate target classes (), researchers develop early indication signals [REF]. This methodology provides interpretable evidence for non-clinical student support within high-stress academic environments [REF].


2.2 Student Mental Health in the School-to-Work Transition
The graduation phase initiates a transition from academic environments to professional settings, representing a period of significant psychological vulnerability [REF]. Early career entrants encounter specific challenges regarding the construction of an occupational identity while navigating initial labor market entry [REF]. Evidence indicates that school leavers experience higher levels of distress during unemployment compared to mature workers with established career histories [REF]. This disparity reflects the added strain of defining a professional self-concept while simultaneously confronting economic instability [REF]. Meta-analytic data confirm that school leavers face an "extra burden" when early career unemployment disrupts the formation of a vocational identity [REF].
Labor market uncertainty acts as a primary stressor during this transition phase [REF]. Perceived job insecurity involves a subjectively experienced anticipation of involuntary job loss, which associates with impaired mental well-being [REF]. This anticipation often generates distress levels comparable to the actual experience of joblessness [REF]. For students, the transition is characterized by prolonged uncertainty, which prevents the utilization of effective coping strategies that are typically available during confirmed employment loss [REF]. The mere expectation of failure in the job search process associates strongly with increased anxiety and depressive symptom severity [REF].
Job search activities during this period constitute a discouraging cycle involving frequent rejection and persistent uncertainty [REF]. Search intensity serves as a predictor of faster reemployment, yet high levels of job-seeking effort associate with lower psychological health during the unemployment interval [REF]. A reciprocal relationship exists where search-related stress increases psychological distress, while this distress may simultaneously motivate continued search effort to end the period of unemployment [REF]. Younger cohorts, typically those under age 40, show more profound associations between labor market stressors and depressive symptoms than older groups [REF]. This sensitivity may relate to the specific financial responsibilities and life-stage transitions characterizing early adulthood [REF].
The deprivation of employment-related benefits further exacerbates psychological strain [REF]. Employment provides manifest rewards like income and latent functions including time structure, social contact, and collective purpose [REF]. Graduating students are deprived of these functions, leading to a state of psychological deprivation [REF]. Meta-analyses of longitudinal data suggest that transitions into unemployment associate with mental health declines, while the shift from school to employment associates with significant gains in well-being [REF]. Consequently, the school-to-work transition identifies as a high-risk phase where academic institutions require indication signals to support students facing psychological vulnerability [REF].
2.3 Job-Search Stressors and Psychological Outcomes
Meta-analyses establish that the subjectively experienced threat of job loss associates with significant declines in psychological and physical health [REF]. Perceived job insecurity functions as a pervasive work stressor, often generating distress levels comparable to the actual state of unemployment [REF]. Standardized odds ratios indicate a 29% elevated risk of depressive symptoms for individuals experiencing insecurity, while currently unemployed persons reveal a 19% increased risk [REF]. Systematic reviews of prospective observational studies confirm that the anticipation of failure serves as a primary stressor, depriving individuals of the manifest and latent functions typically provided by employment [REF]. These latent functions include daily time structure, social interaction networks, and a sense of collective purpose [REF].
The intensity of job-seeking activities during these transitions generates complex psychological outcomes [REF]. While high search effort serves as a predictor of faster reemployment, meta-analytic data suggest search intensity maintains an inverse association with current mental well-being (rc = -0.11) [REF]. This behavioral stressor functions as a discouraging cycle characterized by frequent rejection load and pervasive uncertainty [REF]. Evidence indicates a reciprocal relationship where psychological distress motivates search effort to end the period of unemployment, while the search process simultaneously exacerbates symptom severity [REF]. Specifically, job-seeking activity increases distress because the process involves a high volume of rejection without a guaranteed outcome [REF].
Operational definitions of search strategies significantly influence the comparability of evidence across academic cohorts [REF]. Focused job searches, characterized by clearly defined employment objectives and specific target criteria, associate with higher satisfaction following the transition [REF]. In contrast, haphazard or planless search behaviors correlate with fewer job offers and decreased well-being [REF]. Exploratory search strategies, which involve gathering information about diverse options, associate with a higher volume of offers but do not necessarily enhance post-search satisfaction [REF]. For individuals with low financial resources, the ability to maintain a deliberate search strategy diminishes, as economic pressure often forces the acceptance of lower-quality employment [REF].
Conflicting evidence persists regarding how demographic characteristics moderate the impact of job insecurity [REF]. Initial meta-analyses suggested that occupational status moderated behavioral outcomes but did not influence the association between insecurity and psychological health [REF]. However, updated evaluations using larger databases indicate that older employees and those with longer organizational tenure face more severe health-related consequences from labor market threats [REF]. Conversely, younger cohorts and employees with shorter tenure exhibit more profound turnover intentions when facing insecurity [REF]. These variations likely reflect differences in psychological investment and occupational mobility [REF]. While older workers may face age-related discrimination and possess outdated skills, younger entrants may perceive turnover as a readily available coping mechanism to escape stressful environments [REF].
Measurement methodology also affects the magnitude of reported associations [REF]. Studies utilizing multi-item scales for job insecurity report significantly stronger associations with satisfaction, trust, and performance compared to evaluations relying on single-item assessments [REF]. This discrepancy suggests that single-item metrics with unknown psychometric properties systematically underestimate the magnitude of psychological consequences [REF]. Similarly, perceived financial strain serves as a more reliable predictor of distress than objective measures of financial resources [REF]. This pattern demonstrates that the subjective appraisal of a stressor functions as the primary determinant of current risk state, rather than objective situational metrics [REF]. Longitudinal data reveal that while the risk of mental health problems peaks approximately nine months into the search process, a stabilized state of elevated distress often persists among the long-term unemployed [REF]. Consequently, early indication frameworks must capture both the subjective anticipation of failure and the objective behavioral signals of search effort to provide accurate decision support [REF].


2.4 Social Support as a Protective Factor
Perceived social support signifies a multifaceted external resource generated through relational transactions [REF]. Standardized quantification frequently employs the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) [REF]. Developed initially for undergraduate cohorts, this instrument evaluates subjective adequacy across three independent domains: family, friends, and significant others [REF]. High levels of perceived support associate with decreased symptom severity for both anxiety and depression [REF]. Within student populations, the family subscale maintains a significant inverse correlation with distress indicators [REF].
Mechanism logic differentiates between direct influence and the buffering hypothesis [REF]. While direct influence suggests social integration promotes health across all stress levels, the buffering hypothesis proposes that resources protect individuals specifically during high-strain intervals [REF]. For students navigating employment transitions, these aids modify the internalization of labor market uncertainty [REF]. Quality contacts bolster resilience [REF]. High support levels enhance the propensity of seekers to maintain positive self-evaluations even when encountering persistent rejection [REF]. Conversely, negative interactions or social undermining associate with heightened psychological vulnerability [REF].
Meta-analyses establish that these external aids provide emotional stabilization while also offering direct tangible assistance [REF]. Financial stabilization through close relationships reduces strain derived from income loss [REF]. However, longitudinal data reveal that social resources often diminish during extended periods of joblessness [REF]. This depletion implies a reciprocal relationship where chronic stress eventually erodes the protective systems intended to mitigate it [REF]. Support typically replenishes after successful reemployment [REF].
Integrating these metrics into supervised learning pipelines provides essential context for early risk detection [REF]. Computational models utilize these social features () to account for variance in individual stress resistance [REF]. Most existing frameworks omit these buffering signals, limiting their predictive depth [REF]. Including MSPSS scores enables systems to categorize risk levels with greater precision by acknowledging the presence of protective resources [REF]. Standardized social features ensure risk indication results remain reproducible across diverse academic environments [REF]. Multi-source feature sets facilitate the identification of students facing high search pressures who lack sufficient buffering resources [REF].

These systems automatically identify patterns in large datasets to estimate the probability that a specific risk state exists () [REF], [REF].
Common supervised baselines include support vector machines (), logistic regression (), random forests (), and decision trees () [REF]. Conceptually, these models vary in computational complexity and interpretability [REF]. Decision trees and association rules function as transparent methods where researchers can inspect the step-by-step logic leading to a classification [REF]. In contrast,  and  represent fast, legacy classifiers that frequently demonstrate effective performance but offer less intrinsic visibility into feature interactions [REF]. Neural networks () and deep learning architectures detect complex, non-linear interactions within high-dimensional data [REF]. However, these models often create a black-box phenomenon where the underlying reasoning for an output remains hidden from clinical practitioners [REF], [REF].
Input feature sets originate from distinct modalities, including self-report scores, digital behavioral signals, and linguistic patterns [REF], [REF]. Standardized psychometric tools like the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 provide ground truth labels for many training datasets [REF], [REF]. Behavioral features derive from smartphone or wearable sensor data, capturing objective metrics of physical activity, sleep cycles, and social interaction frequency [REF], [REF]. Linguistic features extracted via natural language processing () analyze sentiment polarity and emotional intensity in unstructured text [REF], [REF]. Transferability concerns persist when deploying risk prediction models across independent student cohorts [REF]. Models trained on social media data frequently encounter demographic biases, as these platforms attract younger users and may not reflect the broader student population [REF], [REF]. Additionally, linguistic signals often depend on language-specific structures; for instance, grammatical indicators in one language may not translate effectively to others, necessitating specialized lexicons [REF], [REF]. Performance often declines when models encounter data from settings that differ from their training environment [REF]. Transfer learning offers a potential mechanism to adapt models for different purposes, yet the heterogeneity of salient mental health data remains a technical barrier [REF].

Consequently, these models serve as early indication aids that require human oversight to ensure output stability in sensitive academic support contexts [REF].





2.6 Explainability, Reproducibility, and Reporting Standards
The opaque nature of deep learning and transformer architectures creates a black-box effect where the rationale for a risk indicator remains hidden [REF], [REF]. For university support services, these hidden logics limit the utility of models for non-clinical interventions because decision-makers cannot verify the feature importance driving a specific risk score [REF]. Evidence establishes that explainable AI is necessary to go beyond black-box methods and justify recommendations to individuals [REF]. Transparent methods allow researchers to inspect the sequential logic of a classification, yet these are often neglected in favor of more complex models with higher raw performance [REF].
High-standard computational research requires adherence to established guidelines like the TRIPOD Statement to ensure steps taken for model development are replicable by other researchers [REF]. This framework demands clear documentation of the target population, predictor definitions, and internal validation techniques used to quantify optimism [REF]. Systematic reviews of prediction research consistently identify poor reporting of missing data handling, statistical techniques, and model building strategies [REF], [REF]. Complementing this, the PROBAST framework identifies risk of bias across four domains: participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis [REF]. Shortcomings in design or conduct often lead to distorted estimates of accuracy and model behavior [REF]. For instance, failure to blind outcome assessors or using inconsistent measurements for predictors introduces substantial bias into a developed model [REF].
Class imbalance presents a significant technical barrier when modeling infrequent occurrences like severe psychological distress or suicide ideation [REF]. Supervised systems often prioritize the identification of the majority class [REF]. By neglecting high-risk individuals in the minority group, this technical bias compromises the reliability of detection in real-world settings [REF]. External validity remains a primary concern as many systems function as early proofs-of-concept with limited validation on independent student cohorts [REF], [REF]. Demographic biases affect models relying on social media signals [REF]. Because younger platform users do not always represent the broader academic population, results may not generalize effectively [REF], [REF]. Implementing internal validation procedures such as bootstrapping or cross-validation is mandatory to account for potential overfitting [REF], [REF]. Model stability depends on performance verification with out-of-sample data [REF].

3.3 Variables and Measurement Instruments
The dependent variables () represent binary or ordinal risk indicators derived from standardized psychometric tools [REF]. Depressive symptom severity is quantified using the PHQ-9 screening tool, which evaluates the frequency of specific symptoms over a 14-day interval [REF]. Each of the nine entries is scored on a 0–3 Likert scale, resulting in a total range of 0 to 27 [REF]. A threshold of 10 or greater identifies high-risk states with a documented sensitivity of 88% [REF]. Anxiety indication is operationalized through the GAD-7 anxiety scale [REF]. This instrument utilizes seven items to produce an aggregate score between 0 and 21 [REF]. For classification purposes, a cut-point of 10 or higher designates the probable risk class, while tiers of 5, 10, and 15 represent mild, moderate, and severe risk levels [REF].
Predictor feature vectors () are organized into three groups to provide multimodal context [REF]. The self-report group incorporates individual Likert-item responses as ordinal features for the model [REF]. The social support group utilizes the MSPSS multi-dimensional scale to quantify external protective resources [REF]. This 12-item measure evaluates subjective adequacy across three independent subscales: family, friends, and significant others [REF]. Items are recorded on a 1–7 scale, and subscale totals function as continuous predictors within the supervised learning baselines [REF].
The job-search behavioral group operationalizes activity patterns during the employment transition [REF]. Intensity of searching reflects the degree of engagement in job-seeking activities, often measured by the frequency of application submissions or active contacts [REF]. Search planfulness defines the strategic orientation of the seeker [REF]. It is categorized into focused searches with specific objectives and haphazard searches which represent planless behavioral patterns [REF]. Rejection load is defined as the total volume of negative responses received by the student [REF]. This variable serves as a primary behavioral stressor associated with the current risk state [REF].
For modeling readiness, data types are assigned based on their distribution properties [REF]. Psychometric totals and social support sub-scores function as continuous variables [REF]. Individual scale responses are treated as ordinal data to preserve relative intensity [REF]. Risk targets are transformed into categorical labels () to enable binary classification and probability estimation () [REF]. This taxonomy ensures the modularity and reproducibility required for student support decision systems [REF].
